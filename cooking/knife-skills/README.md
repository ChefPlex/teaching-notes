# Knife Skills

**Original class:** Underground Food Academy Knife Skills  
**Date:** January 18, 2009  
**Instructor:** Eric White  
**Status:** Archived working reference  
**Source:** [`source/20090113_KnifeSkills.pdf`](source/20090113_KnifeSkills.pdf)

A knife skills class is really a confidence class with sharper consequences.

The original handout covered knife safety, how to hold a knife, how to use a knife and cutting board well, how to chop a variety of vegetables, classic dice sizes, sharpening basics, and enough exercises to make the student realize this is muscle memory, not magic.

## What The Class Was Trying To Teach

The basics are still the right basics:

- Carry the knife safely, blade down and back, and say "knife" when passing behind someone.
- Do not catch a falling knife. Your fingers and toes are more expensive than the knife. Let gravity finish its little speech.
- Keep the knife parallel to the edge of the table when it is not in use.
- Put a wet cloth under the cutting board so the board does not skate around the table.
- Set up containers before you start chopping, because future-you deserves fewer tiny piles of onion on the counter.
- Use a pinch grip near the bolster, not a death grip on the handle.
- Keep the knife in contact with the board and use the forward-down, back-again motion for control.
- Steel the knife while working and sharpen it when it needs sharpening. Dull knives do not make you safer. They make you inventive in bad ways.
- Use the spine of the knife, not the edge, when scraping the board. Better yet, use a bench scraper.
- Cut things to similar sizes when they need to cook at the same rate.

## Class Flow

### 1. Safety

The safety rules come first because the room gets much less fun when someone learns the phrase "urgent care" before they learn small dice.

Focus on how people move through the kitchen, where the knife rests when not in use, and what to do when a knife falls.

### 2. Station Setup

A stable cutting board, enough room, containers nearby, and the right knife for the task are not niceties. They are the difference between cooking and chasing celery across a table.

This is also where mise en place belongs. The original handout defines it as getting everything together and putting it where you can reach it before you start. That is still the good answer.

### 3. The Chef's Knife

The handout explains the parts of the knife and the basic pinch grip. The important teaching point is fit. If a knife is not comfortable in the student's hand, try another knife. The best knife is the one the student can control safely.

### 4. Cutting Motion

The teaching image shows the knife moving with the blade in contact with the cutting board. The core idea is control: tip down, forward and down, then back again.

### 5. Dice Sizes

Dice size is not culinary trivia. It controls cooking time, texture, and whether the ingredient disappears into the dish or remains visible at the end.

Classic sizes from the handout:

| Cut | Approximate size |
|---|---:|
| Fine dice / brunoise | 1/8 inch square |
| Small dice / macedoine | 1/4 inch square |
| Medium dice | 1/3 inch square |
| Large dice | 3/4 inch square |
| Cube | Greater than 3/4 inch square |

### 6. Practice

The original class exercises are very practical: celery, onion, carrot, mushrooms, potato, citrus, green pepper. This is the right mix because the student gets repetition across shapes, textures, skin, and resistance.

## Before Teaching This Again

Update the equipment links and product recommendations. They were useful in 2009, but knife-buying advice ages faster than knife technique.

Also, decide whether this is a historical archive or a refreshed class. If it becomes a refreshed class, add photos or short videos of the grip, board setup, scraping with the spine, and the cutting motion. Those are the moments where people nod in class and then forget the second they are alone with an onion.
