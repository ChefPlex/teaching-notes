This is a talk about Louis Pasteur, but really it's about a recurring human pattern: someone tries to solve a practical problem, does enough careful looking, and accidentally opens a door much larger than the one they meant to walk through.

Pasteur was trying to help with wine spoilage. The bigger story ends up running through fermentation, microbes, pasteurization, food safety, vaccines, scientific credit, public health, industrial process, and brunch. Because history is apparently incapable of staying in its lane.

## The Thesis

He was not trying to save humanity. He was trying to save wine.

But once you understand that spoilage isn't bad vibes, bad air, or the universe being rude to a barrel, the whole system changes. You can identify microorganisms. You can use controlled heat. You can standardize time and temperature. You can make trade safer. You can make milk less terrifying. You can start thinking about disease differently.

Not bad for a French chemist with a microscope and an attitude problem.

## Talk Arc

The full outline is in [`outline.md`](outline.md).

1. **The hook** - Louis Pasteur as the person who made it possible to drink wine without playing bacterial roulette.
2. **France's boozy crisis** - wine and beer were spoiling, exports were suffering, and money tends to make governments suddenly interested in science.
3. **Chance favors the prepared mind** - the famous observation line, which works because the talk is partly about accidents that are only useful when someone is ready to notice them.
4. **Fermentation science** - yeast is not decomposition. Different microorganisms produce different outcomes. One path gets you alcohol. Another gets you sour, ruined wine.
5. **Napoleon III and the wine industry** - the practical commission that pulls Pasteur into the problem.
6. **Germ theory** - invisible organisms causing visible problems, which sounds obvious now and sounded like madness to plenty of people then.
7. **The bright idea** - heat the liquid, kill the troublemakers, cool it down, and profit, literally.
8. **Pasteurization beyond wine** - beer, milk, trade, shelf stability, and the unglamorous public health work that keeps people from meeting the porcelain gods.
9. **Vaccines** - chicken cholera, anthrax, rabies, and the useful but sometimes over-polished legends around scientific discovery.
10. **Hero or PR king** - Pasteur as brilliant, petty, competitive, reputation-aware, and still historically enormous.
11. **The toast** - raise your glass to the person who made drinking safer, brunch possible, and a lot of modern microbiology easier to explain at parties.

## What Works

The frame works. Start with wine. Keep the audience in the room with booze, microbes, and food safety. Then walk them into germ theory and vaccines before they realize they have been tricked into learning history of science.

The humor also works because it's not separate from the explanation. It is the handle on the explanation.

## Before Presenting This Again

Do a source pass.

The deck has a strong story, but several claims should be verified and footnoted before using it in a formal educational setting, especially:

- exact dates and temperature ranges for wine, beer, and milk pasteurization
- foodborne illness and trade impact numbers
- chicken cholera vaccine details and how much of the vacation story is legend versus documented work
- Toussaint, anthrax, and the chemical inactivation issue
- Pasteur's rivalry and reputation management sections

The current version is good as a lively talk archive. A refreshed version should add a source list and separate the punchlines from the claims that need citations.
